The idea in three lines

Repeatable, not bespoke

Every use case follows one risk-based path. Review depth depends on type, data sensitivity and impact.

Controls inside the workflow

Guardrails, evaluations, monitoring and human-in-the-loop rules are built into AI platforms and delivery pipelines, so each control leaves evidence.

Trust earned with data

People approve where risk or regulation requires it, deciding from an objective evidence packet. Decision time and agreement are tracked over time.

Where to go next
One program, four risk profiles

Models, copilots, agents and RAG workflows need different controls

The governance path is the same for all four, but what reviewers and monitors look for changes. Agents and RAG workflows need the closest attention, because they act on tools and depend on the content they retrieve. This is how I would extend the governance I built into AI-assisted and agentic engineering to all four. "Typical today" describes a typical enterprise, not any specific company.

A policyholder filing a disability claim

The same six stages on this journey. Each card leads with the customer moment, then the agent action, then the control. Hard human sign-offs are marked; everything else is governance by exception.

1 · Discover

Customer moment

Policyholder needs clarity on eligibility, timeline and next steps after an unexpected stop from work.

Agent: journey agent maps goal, intent and baseline; surfaces the end-to-end bet (intake → decision → payment).

Control · B Build: business owner registers use case, purpose and risk tier. Partner and in-house share one outcome scorecard.

2 · Design

Customer moment

Claimant should file once, see status, and know a person decides denials.

Agent: creator agents draft MVP and BDD stories from the journey KPI; judge agent flags gaps before humans review.

Control · B Build: product + claims lead approve stories and tool scope (read-only first). DFS / NAIC requirements written into the design.

3 · Build

Customer moment

Intake, identity, document gather and medical review must work as one path.

Agent: code, test and review agents implement intake runtime, RAG over policy docs, MCP tools, A2A medical handoff.

Control · C Control: engineers own schemas and safe tool lists; health data masked; out-of-scope actions blocked.

4 · Evaluate

Customer moment

Before go-live, the path must be as fair and grounded for this claimant as for the last hundred.

Agent: judge / eval agents replay past claims; grounding, fairness and non-functional checks.

Control · E Evaluate: QE + risk sign the eval packet. Failures become new test cases — not exceptions to skip.

5 · Release · HUMAN SIGN-OFF

Customer moment

Examiner opens one screen: recommended benefit, duration, rationale and evidence — then decides.

Agent: assembles the evidence packet; payment and notice tools stay locked until approval. Denials never auto-fire.

Control · A Approve: examiner decides. This is a hard human sign-off. Routine low-risk patterns elsewhere use governance by exception — review depth rises on alerts, not every transaction (Steve Rullo, Insurance Business, Oct 9, 2026).

6 · Run & improve · AUTO-CHECKED

Customer moment

Claimant is paid and notified; the next claimant should get a better experience from what this one taught.

Agent: observe agents trace every step; drift and incident alerts; examiner edits become new test cases.

Control · O Observe / N Nurture: ops + risk own open exceptions with owners and dates. Skills converge on the same journey KPI.

Human sign-off vs governance by exception

People stay accountable. The system earns their trust with evidence.

On this claim journey, the examiner’s decision is a hard human sign-off — denials never auto-fire. Everywhere else, review depth follows governance by exception: people are flagged on alerts and deviations rather than every transaction, as Steve Rullo has said publicly (Insurance Business, Oct 9, 2026). The person decides from an evidence packet; time and agreement are tracked.

The reviewer can open any line of evidence and records Approve, Reject or Ask for changes with a reason. Time to decision is logged.

Review depth, by use case pattern
  1. Full reviewEvery new pattern starts here, with a complete human review of design, data and evaluations.
  2. Evidence-packet reviewThe reviewer decides from the packet and opens detail when something looks off. The floor for customer-facing and decision-supporting AI.
  3. Fast-path approvalFor a pattern with 97%+ agreement over 200+ decisions and no incidents: the packet arrives pre-filled, a person still approves, and a sample gets a deep review.

A person decides at every level. Any incident, or agreement dropping below the threshold, moves the pattern back a level automatically. To guard against rubber-stamping, a blind sample of cases is reviewed independently each month, and any drop in independent agreement moves the pattern back a level.

In a live walkthrough I’d walk examiner time-to-decide and agreement rate against the journey KPIs — no invented year-one targets here.

Disagreements are the most useful data: each one is reviewed, and either the person caught something the checks missed (so a check is added) or the check was right (so the packet is made clearer).

What broke, and what I changed

During my own hands-on testing of our AI-first PDLC, I found AI-generated code exposing the full server directory structure in an error response instead of a safe error. Security testing ran static scans (SAST) and only positive dynamic tests (DAST), because test data for failure cases wasn’t available. We added negative scenarios to DAST. That uncovered a second risk: log files held details that were being exposed to AI models. Both became permanent pipeline checks, with safe error handling and log scrubbing before any data reaches a model, so approvers decided from evidence instead of chasing it, and approval time on regulated checks went from 3 days to same day.
